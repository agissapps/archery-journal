String elbow

Where the string-arm elbow sits at full draw and why it matters.

Elbow on the line

The elbow ends up on the line of force: the back holds the string and the arm works little [1]. You get there by moving the shoulder blade toward the spine and taking the elbow back [2].

Elbow off the line

The elbow leaves the line and the arm has to hold the force. A sideways force appears on the hand [1]. As an example, the source calculates that a 37 mm sideways error in the elbow, with 40 pounds of holding force, gives about 3 kg of side force on the hand [1].

What it is

It is the elbow of the arm that pulls the string. Where it sits at full draw decides whether the string is held by the big back muscles or the small arm muscles [2][1].

You draw by pulling the string elbow back in one fluid motion, with the string-side shoulder blade moving toward the spine. Doing it with the back muscles puts both arms in a straight line pointing at the target [2].

The line of force

Bow International, with Dr. James Park's analysis, explains it with a “line of force”: it runs from the bow hand to the string position (the middle finger, on a recurve). Seen from above it lines up with the arrow; seen from the side, it does not [1].

The ideal is for the elbow joint to sit on that line. Then the back holds the bow, and the forearm sits a little above the arrow line rather than lined up with it [1].

If the elbow is off the line, the biceps or triceps have to hold the arm in a weak, fully flexed position. It is tiring and unsteady. Under-drawing is a common cause [1].

Errors and how to fix them

This is what the sources say, in small steps and best with a coach watching you:

Pulling with the arm
If the elbow bends first and the arm pulls the string, think of the back first and then move the elbow back [2]. Starting the draw by rotating the string-side shoulder blade and taking the elbow back gives good back tension, consistent form and less tiring [2].
Elbow off the line
Set your draw length so the elbow sits on the line of force; the string then sits along the side of the jaw. Keep the shoulders in the same position every shot and check with side and overhead photos [1].
Wrist and fingers
Wrist and finger joints should stay straight from draw to release. Flexing them is tiring and less accurate. You can see it by watching the wrist just after the release [1].
To practice the motion
Mimic the T-form shot with no arrow to learn the movement. Never release with no arrow in the bow [2].
