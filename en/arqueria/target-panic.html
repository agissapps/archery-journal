Target panic

When the shot locks up or fires by itself right as you aim: how to recognize it and how it's worked on.

Pick a form and see how it looks on the target

Forcing

A typical example: your pin wanders around and, when it passes the middle, you push the shot. On compound it's called “punching” [1].

Won't rise

You can't get the pin onto the gold: it stays low, no matter what you do [1].

Won't release

You get to the middle and don't release. You hold, shake and let the bow down.

Zap!

The arrow goes the moment the pin touches the gold, before you decide to release.

This split into four is the one we use in our videos. World Archery talks about three forms: difficulty releasing, not being able to put the sight on the middle, and releasing too early [1]. “Forcing” and “Zap!” both fit in that last one.

What it is

It is a difficulty executing the shot right as you aim: not being able to release, not being able to put the sight on the middle, or releasing too early [1].

Wikipedia describes it through three symptoms: premature anchor (the bow feels very heavy and full anchor is hard to reach), premature hold (the archer “locks up” while lining the arrow up with the target) and premature release (can't reach full anchor without releasing) [2].

It used to be called “gold panic” because it showed up when aiming at the gold. The name changed because it happens on any target [2].

As for the cause: it used to be blamed on anxiety and fear of failing. Today it is thought to have to do with how the brain learns and remembers movements [2][1]. That explanation is still debated, not a closed case. Another source treats it as cognitive anxiety (overthinking) and somatic anxiety (a revved-up body) [3].

It happens even to elite archers, and World Archery says it can be managed [1].

What to do

These steps are the base that several sources repeat. They go in order:

  1. Name itRecognizing it is the first step [4].
  2. Take the target awayShoot at a blank bale, with no target and no aiming, eyes open or closed, and very close to the bale [5][4]. The goal is to execute the shot without thinking about the middle.
  3. Come back to the target slowlyFirst with no sight, then through an empty ring, then at a big target up close. Move back and shrink the center little by little [5][4].
  4. AlternateOne arrow at the blank bale and one at the target, to carry a smooth release feeling to the target [5].
  5. If it returns, go back to step 2It's normal. The answer is to return to the blank bale [4].

Depending on how it shows up

The sources don't tie each drill to a specific form, except one that does [5]. Here is what each says and where it makes sense to start; take it as our suggestion, not a recipe.

Forcing
If you use a finger trigger, some archers switch to a back-tension release, because it removes the trigger you can “punch” [6]. (We couldn't open that page, so this is unverified.) At World Archery, one archer says changing his grip on the bow or release helped [1].
Won't rise
Remove the dot from the sight pin and enlarge the ring (and the peep, on compound): the sight picture gets wider and you stop over-aiming. And practice up close at a big target, shrinking the center little by little [5].
Won't release
Alternate blank bale and target, to carry to the target the feeling that the clicker or release lets go smoothly [5]. Korean coach Kim Hyung Tak, according to World Archery, removes all the stabilizer weight, moves to a lighter bow and rebuilds strength little by little [1].
Zap!
A fixed shot routine with controlled breathing helps you not rush the moment. One source describes breathing in for 6 seconds, holding 2 and breathing out 7, plus mental imagery of the shot and practice with clear goals [3]. It is the case of a single archer, not a proven protocol.

When to get help

If you don't improve after a while, work with a coach and, if needed, a sports psychologist [4][3].

It can take weeks and it can come back; when it does, go back to the blank bale [4]. Pushing through it without fixing it builds bad habits [4].

In the app: To count your shots at the blank bale, use the app's Volume mode.

Heads up: This is general information, not medical or psychological treatment. The sources are blogs, shops and archery media, and several describe individual cases. We couldn't verify the details of the Lancaster source (distances and duration), so we don't give them.
