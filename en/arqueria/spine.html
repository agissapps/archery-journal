Spine

Arrow stiffness, how it's measured and why it has to match your bow.

Stiff (340)

With 880 g at the center, the arrow bends little: 0.340 of an inch. That's why its spine is 340 [1][2].

Medium (500)

It bends 0.500 of an inch. Spine 500 [1][2].

Weak (700)

It bends a lot, 0.700 of an inch: spine 700. A high number, a weak arrow [1][2].

An example with round numbers to show how to read it; real arrows come with their spine marked. Easton supports the arrow at two points 28 inches apart and uses an 880-gram weight [1][2].

What it is

Spine is the calibrated stiffness of the arrow shaft. The standard measuring method comes from Doug Easton, in the 1940s [1].

A higher number means a weaker arrow, because it bends more. A 500 spine is weaker than a 400 [1][2].

Dynamic spine

The marked spine is the static one. What matters in the shot is how the arrow behaves as it leaves, the dynamic spine, and it changes with several factors [1][2].

Easton, talking about a compound bow, says the arrow behaves stiffer with a lower peak bow weight, a lighter point, a different point and insert combination, a heavier string material, heavier vanes, heavier serving, a nocking point change or a shorter arrow [2].

How to choose yours

Easton recommends using its chart or selector with your bow's real measurements: measure your draw weight and draw length instead of guessing. It says guessing is the main reason for wrong choices [2].

An arrow that is too weak veers right and one that is too stiff tends to fly slightly left, according to Easton [2]. To see how you detect it in practice, see “The archer's paradox” [3].

In the app: Save your arrow sets with their spine, length and point, and pick which one you shoot.

Heads up: Easton's sources are partly aimed at compound and hunting bows; for recurve, use your manufacturer's chart. We don't give a table of numbers: it depends on each archer's draw weight, length and point.
