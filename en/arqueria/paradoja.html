The archer's paradox

Why the arrow bends as it leaves, how it clears the bow and what happens if it isn't the right one.

Right

The arrow bends around the riser without touching it, swings from side to side and straightens out until it reaches the middle [1][2].

Too stiff

It doesn't bend enough: it is forced off to one side as the string nears the riser [2]. With a right-handed archer and a bare shaft, it lands left of the group [3].

Too weak

It deforms too much and is sent to the other side of the target [2]. With a right-handed archer and a bare shaft, it lands right [3]. In extreme cases the arrow can break [2].

The curve is exaggerated and the drawing is not to scale. It is the view from above for a right-handed archer; for left-handers it's the opposite [3]. The sources don't say how many times it oscillates or at what distance it straightens, so we don't state it.

What it is

The paradox is this: the arrow goes where you pointed at full draw, even though it seems it would have to pass through the position it had before drawing, when it pointed to one side of the target [2].

The bending of the arrow on release is what explains the paradox. It shouldn't be confused with the paradox itself [2].

The name was first used in 1913 (E. J. Rendtroff), although a text by Horace A. Ford from 1859 already describes the phenomenon. Clarence Hickman was the first to film the arrow flexing around the bow [2].

Why it bends

When you release, the string pushes on the back of the arrow. That end gets the energy first and moves faster than the tip. The arrow can't react instantly, so it absorbs part of that energy by bending a little before it leaves [1].

On a recurve, the string has to travel around the fingers as it returns, which adds a horizontal push, so the bend is sideways. That's why a pressure button is used: it helps the arrow pass the riser and leave straight [1]. Wikipedia sums it up this way: mainly the way the string is deflected as it leaves the fingers [2].

On a compound, since no fingers touch the string, there is no sideways push and the arrow moves up and down instead [1].

The farther it gets from the bow, the straighter it flies, because less energy goes into bending. Fletching helps it straighten sooner because it slows the back end, which moves faster [1].

Every arrow in flight has two nodes, one near the back and one near the front, and the straight line between them points where it is heading [1].

On a centre-shot riser the arrow shows no paradoxical behavior, although it still flexes a little, because it visually points along its line of flight [2].

The bare-shaft test

It is the practical way to find out whether your arrow is the right one. The steps, in order:

  1. Prepare the bare shaftCut the fletching off one arrow with a sharp knife. For best results, weigh the fletching and stick painter's tape of the same weight where it was [3].
  2. ShootShoot the bare shaft together with three fletched arrows, at about 9 meters (10 yards), with good shots, especially the bare shaft [3].
  3. Read the heightIf the bare shaft lands low, the nocking point is too high; if it lands high, it is too low [3].
  4. Read the sideRight-handed archer: if it lands left of the group, the spine is too stiff; if it lands right, it is too weak. Left-handed, the opposite [3].
  5. Adjust and repeatChange one thing in small increments and shoot again. Then step back to about 14 meters (15 yards) and repeat until the bare shaft groups with the others [3].

What to adjust

Fix the height first (step 3) and the side after. These are the changes the source proposes, always in small increments [3]:

Too stiff
Increase point weight, increase draw weight, use longer arrows or buy weaker-spine arrows (a higher number) [3].
Too weak
Decrease point weight, decrease draw weight, buy stiffer-spine arrows (a lower number) or shorten the arrow by a quarter inch (about 6 mm) [3]. Our suggestion: shortening only works as long as the arrow doesn't end up shorter than the point where it touches the clicker or the arrow rest (on a traditional bow, the shelf). If it would end up shorter, don't cut it: buy other arrows.
